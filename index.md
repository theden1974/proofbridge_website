# proofbridge · Dennis Jansen

## I find the real problem before you build the wrong solution.

Proofbridge connects the AI, digital and data world with real, actual ground work: being in the field, validating the theoretical world.

### 1. The underlying problem.
The wrong people often have the mandate for the solution, without understanding the underlying problem. When somebody starts with a solution, I listen, and come back with the underlying issue by asking the why of the direction.
- Seen in the field: Elderly care. The funding was for an app. In the field, the elderly were not good at mobiles and apps. The concept without an app was not built out: the money was spent for the wrong reasons.

### 2. Knowledge is not always truth.
Intelligence and reasoning are useless when there is no validated real-world data, validated for its purpose. AI doesn't validate; AI rephrases.
- Seen in the field: Healthcare. The task was app screens for dietitians. The question that mattered: are your AI models based on the right data? A patient's weight has many input points: doctor, home, nurse, morning, afternoon.

### 3. The bullshit, caught from an expert point of view.
Only an expert can audit the output. Outside your expertise, everything keeps sounding reasonable.
- Seen in the field: Legal. An architect's AI summary of a 60-page document sounded legit. The referenced paragraphs were off. It would never stand before court.

### Method
- Real-life experiencing: seeing the actual product at work, seeing the problems and spotting the underlying issues while using the product, the platform or seeing the customer interaction.
- Interviews, to separate the must-haves from the nice-to-haves.
- There is always a translation between the stakeholder that thinks what is needed and the actual user that can express or audit what is needed.
- The output is audited by the actual stakeholder for whom the problem occurs. I just translate.

### Evidence from the field
### Elderly care
- Starting point: Funding of 1 million euros, to help long-term unemployed people work in the care of the elderly. They already decided they probably needed a mobile phone app.
- What I saw: I asked if I could interview the people they already deployed and the elderly still living independently at home. The elderly were indeed not good at mobiles and apps. They wanted to be self-sufficient.
- After: I created a concept called zorg concierge: no app for the elderly, only an app for the employee working in the flat. It was not built out. They scaled the elderly care homes solution instead.

### Healthcare
- Starting point: The task was optimising app screens for dietitians.
- What I saw: It was not so much the dietitian that needed the right app or platform to enter data, but making the patient the centre of the concept.
- After: My follow-up question: how do you know if your AI models are based on the right data (entries)? A patient's weight over time has so many input points: doctor, at home, nurse, morning, afternoon.

### Water authority
- What I saw: As far as I have seen or understood, the permit officer is there to fill a system with data. Ask what happens with a data point and it is not about the purpose of the data point but the place it goes next: department x, y, z.
- After: The purpose of the data point, how it can be applied, when, why and in what capacity, is not clear. (Translated from Dutch.)

### Legal, architecture
- Starting point: An architect used AI to summarise a 60-page legal document. The output seemed legit.
- What I saw: He checked the output against the referenced paragraphs and they were off.
- After: When brought to a legal case it wouldn't hold.

### Building with AI
- What I saw: The failure that keeps coming back is my inability to interface with the chat in such a way that I can draw the outline of what I expect it to do and how I expect the system to work. There are constant misalignments. And I noticed the lock-in of the different models, and the direction it is heading.
